# Changelog

All notable changes to this project are documented here. The format follows [Keep a Changelog](https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/), and versions follow [Semantic Versioning](https://semver.org/).

## [Unreleased]

### Added

- **The score at a glance.** `review` and `round` open their summary with three short tables counted from the project: the parts that sound in each section (attacked there or held into it) with their notes a bar, how often the chord changes by section, and the melody's rhythm (syncopated attacks and pushes, the longest stretch without a rest of half a beat, rests by section). Each figure is printed with its rule. They describe the score for an agent that cannot hear it; none is a question or a mark. `score_review.json` holds them in full under `views` ([outputs.md](skills/music-production/references/outputs.md)).
- **Every review question has an identifier** (`questions` in `score_review.json`: `vertical`, `outside_chord.melody`, `sections_alike:verse+chorus` and the like), which stays the same however the question is worded. With `--previous`, a round lists the questions that are new since the round before and those that are gone (`new_questions`, `gone_questions` in `round.json`), so a fix that raises another question is seen in the round that made it.
- **The plan is data, and the score is read against it.** A project can carry a `plan` ([schema.md](skills/music-production/references/schema.md)): what identifies the piece, its high point and by what means, and for each section what the composer commits to: the parts that sound, the part that leads and those that answer, a part's register, the harmony's pace and the dynamics against the section before, and a sentence of their own on what sets the section apart. Every field is optional. `review` reads the score against each commitment and asks where they differ, with the figures; a high point's means are shown and judged by nothing. `round` keeps the plan as first written and says where a later plan departs from it. A project without a plan is asked for one, and nothing refuses it.
- **A decision can answer a question.** A decision that begins with a question's identifier (`"stiffness:keys: a sequenced arpeggio, meant"`) answers it: the review lists it with the reason under `answered` and does not ask it again.
- **A question names its notes.** A collision is printed with the ids of its two notes, and a note outside the chord with its id and, where it is held into the chord, the bar and beat where it was struck: the question named the chord's beat, where no note is written.
- **Each piece starts from words of its own.** Given the same request twice, an agent wrote much the same piece twice: three symphonic pieces made from one sentence shared a form, a path through the keys and a word of their titles. `studio.py words <project>` now draws twelve words at random for a piece, once, from a list of a thousand (`references/association-words.tsv`), and keeps the draw beside the project. The request is settled first and nothing changes what it asks for; then the request and all twelve words are linked into one whole, a scene or a story or a chain of associations, and that whole decides what the request left open, the title included (SKILL.md, step 2). The project keeps the words, the whole and what it gave the music (`association` in [schema.md](skills/music-production/references/schema.md)); `review` asks for them and refuses nothing. A user gives words of their own with `--own`, or none with `--none`; a revision keeps the piece's words. In the trials, two pieces from one request shared about half as many chord moves and melody rhythms with the words as without (score counts on three to five pieces a group; nobody claims the pieces are better for it, and the words did not move a model's most settled defaults: all six symphonic pieces still centred on D).
- **How much harmony rock and chart songs use**, in the harmony guide: chord changes a bar, the number of different chords and roots in a song, root motion and kinds of chord, counted on the RS 200 (de Clercq and Temperley 2011) and the McGill Billboard annotations (Burgoyne, Wild and Fujinaga 2011). Counts, not targets; the credit is in [THIRD_PARTY.md](THIRD_PARTY.md).
- **Next to no hiss under quiet notes: two pianos, then every instrument heard to need it.** A soft, pedalled passage carried the recordings' hiss with it: 50 dB under the music at 12 to 20 kHz on the Steinway and 60 on the Osiris piano. It now lies 75 and 76 dB under it, and the levels are what they were.
  - The Steinway's soft and middle dynamics are made from its loud one, key by key (`vcsl` build b8, from b7): after the hammer leaves the string a piano rings as a linear system, and a harder blow gives the upper partials more (Hall and Askenfelt 1988; Askenfelt and Jansson 1993), so the loud take is given the soft blow's level partial by partial and carries its own noise, lowered. The loud dynamic is bit for bit that of b4.
  - The Osiris piano has each band of a take from 1 kHz up taken out once it has come down to the hiss, once and never back, and its takes stored at full scale and attenuated as much (`piano_osiris` build b6): the hiss and the 16-bit dither were about as loud as each other, so either step alone gained 3 dB and both 16. Its takes' early levels are those of b3 within 0.02 dB.
  - A count of every decaying take of the banks then found hiss under the ringing notes of thirty-one more instruments. Each was played softly, alone and dry, and heard, and the bands of these are closed the same way: the nylon guitar (`iowa_guitar` b4), the folk harp (`vcsl_world` b3), the concert harp, vibraphone, tubular bells and timpani (`vcsl` b8), the Kawai grand, the Yamaha and Knight uprights, the TX81Z presets and the French and Flemish harpsichords (`vcsl_keys` b4) and the VSCO upright (`vsco_colors` b5). In the opening of a piece for guitar, harp and plucked basses the quietest moments measured 18 dB less at 6 to 12 kHz and 27 dB less at 12 to 20 kHz. In each bank every preset not named is bit for bit what it was, and the early level of every changed take is within 0.02 dB (one soft guitar take, 0.35 dB). The glockenspiel was tried and is as recorded: each band of its takes holds a partial of the bar that rings on, or a steady tone of the recording. The pizzicato presets were tried, gained next to nothing and are as recorded: their takes are short, and what a section's takes hold besides the note is the other players' strings, which closing bands does not remove.
  - The closing never touches a note's first 0.06 s, a take whose peak or level it would lower by more than 0.5 dB stays as recorded, and no band closes while a narrow component its filter would take from is heard in the hiss: it closes after the last quarter second that holds one, and stays as recorded where one lasts into the later part of the take. The first two came from the builds' own checks (a first build closed the bands over 8 kHz through the blow, and a take that begins with a touch of the string and sounds its note later read as settled in between); the third from an independent review of the code, which showed a slowly dying partial removed as hiss, and from the banks built after it, where the FM voices' partials had gone the same way because their top band holds partials too. The banks named above are the builds made with all three.
  - `soft_from`, `close_hiss` and `full_scale` are options of a bank's build spec ([soundbank-building.md](skills/music-production/references/soundbank-building.md)); a spec without them builds the file it built before. What they leave: a soft take's own decay and the sound of its blow (the Steinway's two made dynamics have the loud take's), and, where bands are closed, the hiss of a band while its partials still sound, everything under 1 kHz (a mains hum included), room sounds that are no hiss, and takes under 2 s ([noise.md](skills/music-production/references/noise.md)). An installed copy keeps its banks until `fetch_soundbanks.py` is run again.
- **Which harpsichord.** The baroque guide names the Flemish 8′ (`vcsl_keys` 1:6) as the harpsichord to use and the 8′+4′ (2:6) for two registers together; it listed four without a word on which. Program 6 with no bank named gives the French, some of whose takes carry sounds from outside the instrument late in their ring.
- **Which piano, which preset, how near** ([piano-and-chamber.md](skills/music-production/references/piano-and-chamber.md)): the Steinway grand (`vcsl` 0:0) for every piano part, a solo piece and the piano of a band or an orchestra alike, made brighter in the mix where a part wants it and not by changing the piano; another piano only where the user names it or the piece is built on its sound (a worn upright for lo-fi, the Osiris's closeness in slow, sparse music, its pedal preset only where the whole instrument should ring, since each note brings the pedal's own sounds). With the banks installed the review asks about a piano part on any other piano (`piano:<part>`), and a decision that begins with the identifier answers it. The style guides name the Steinway where they named other pianos. And a clarity for a solo piano (`mix.c80_db`, about +2 to +3 dB), since a piano left at its sends alone is heard as dry.

### Changed

- **Seven new examples take the place of the four.** Each was made with 0.6.0 in one take, from one message: an opening that is the same for all seven and a brief of three or four sentences ([examples/prompt.md](examples/prompt.md)). *Stormglass Gambit* and *Cairnfire* are by Claude Fable 5.2, *Glass Towers, Low Gear* and *Fughetta in D minor* by Claude Opus 5.5, *Paper Lantern Hours* by Claude Sonnet 5.5, *The Stone Vault Turns to Light* by GPT-6 Astra and *Skybutton Speedway* by GPT-6 Luna. Nobody replied to an agent while it worked, nothing was edited afterwards, and none was picked from several. Each folder has the brief, the agent's script and the project as rendered; the recordings are the release examples-4, and compositions and recordings are CC0.
- **Where the skill runs is written down.** `SKILL.md` and the readmes say what it needs (an agent that runs commands on your computer, internet access for the setup, a folder that stays: about 1 GB, about 12 GB with every bank) and where it is tested (Claude Code and Codex). An agent that cannot do the setup where it is now says so instead of starting it.
- `fetch_soundbanks.py --only examples` installs the banks the new examples use: `bass_sneaky` and `organ_vcsl` are in the group, `piano_osiris` and `vcsl_winds` no longer.

### Fixed

- **Found by an independent review of this version's code before its release, and fixed in it**, each with its input in the selfcheck (`the_code_review_of_0_7_stays_fixed`), which was then run against the code broken in 23 ways and failed each time:
  - a bank's zone attenuation over what the file holds (57.6 dB as heard) was cut to the limit without a word, so a take stored at full scale under a large `attenuation_db` played up to 18 dB too loud: the build stops and says so;
  - a soft take made with a curve borrowed from another key was named by that key alone, so two layers that borrow different curves of it stored one sample for both; and a take shared by a `full_scale` layer and a plain one was stored once, as whichever came first;
  - a decision answered every question whose identifier it began with (one for the section `answer: reprise` also answered the question of `answer`): it answers the longest;
  - a round whose project had no plan lost the plan as first written, so the plan written again a round later passed for the first: it is carried on, and the round says the plan is gone;
  - two harmony entries at one beat with one symbol passed validation and stopped `review`; a write of the piece's words that failed part way left half a record that every later run refused, and a record that was no record was read as one;
  - `doctor` gave Surge XT's version where the plugin was not at its configured path; the harmony's pace was compared as rounded (1/3 and 3/8 of a change a bar counted as a step apart); with a change of tempo the melody's longest run was the one of most beats, shown with its seconds.
- `SKILL.md`, steps 1 and 2: step 1 writes down what the request says and lists what it leaves open; step 2 decides what is open, after the words are drawn. Step 1 read as if it settled everything.
- `doctor` says whether Surge XT is set up, with its version and the number of patches it finds, or that synth parts written for it will play their built-in fallbacks. It listed the SoundFonts and said nothing of the synthesizer, so a setup that had come out without it looked complete.
- `studio.py` names a Python without NumPy as that, with the interpreter's path and what to do; every command of it stopped on a traceback, and an agent told to install nothing had no way to tell the user what was missing.
- The CI's Windows job, which installs Surge XT, now fails where the setup came out without it (`MUSIC_SKILL_REQUIRE_SURGE`, as `MUSIC_SKILL_REQUIRE_OGG` does for the Ogg encoder); it passed on the built-in fallback alone.
- `voice_lead` says when a range leaves a chord no voicing that keeps its spacing rules. It then took one that breaks them, as its notes describe, and said nothing: four voices of a ninth chord in fourteen semitones came back as a cluster with a minor second in it. It now names the chord and the range in a warning (`voicing.GivenUp`) and what to change: a wider range, a lower `low`, or fewer voices.
- The selfcheck's `a_killed_render_resumes` no longer fails by chance. It kills a render while the MP3 is checked; the render makes the MP3, the FLAC and a game loop together, so the kill could land in the instant the loop's file was made and not yet noted in the journal, and the resume then stopped on that file, as it is meant to. The renders the check kills now make those files one at a time. The renderer is unchanged.

## [0.6.0] - 2026-11-11

A true-peak limiter in the master, a lead that stays near where it is written, and a timing line after how players keep to a beat.

### Added

- **The mix's rules of version 2** (`mix.rules: 2` on the bus chain; [performance-and-mix.md](skills/music-production/references/performance-and-mix.md#the-rules-of-version-2-mixrules-2)). A project without the key stays on version 1 and is mixed as it was. None of what version 2 changes is set from a measurement of the piece's audio: each takes what it needs from the project file. What version 1 measures, version 2 still does (each part to its written level, the mix to its loudness target, a wide master's `range_lu`).
  - The lead bus's compressor takes its threshold from the lead's written level: 6 dB over the loudest lead part, with 0.4 dB of make-up. Under version 1 one threshold served every piece, so the compressor took between 1.2 and 8.9 dB from the lead bus's loudness across 27 test projects and the lead bus came out between 4.8 dB under and 2.8 dB over the sum of its parts. The same projects rendered under version 2: of the 21 whose lead bus compresses, the bus ends within 0.4 dB of the sum of its parts on 20, and 1.1 dB under on one, a bell line whose peaks stand far over its level. These are this skill's test projects, not a bound. The lead is compressed less than under version 1, which is a difference in sound and is to be heard.
  - One limiter on true peaks in the master (`musiclib/limiter.py`, after Hämäläinen 2002), with no delay. Pushed 6 dB into limiting it leaves a 50 Hz tone's harmonics 115 dB down where FFmpeg's limiter as version 1 sets it leaves them 37 dB down, and it reaches the true-peak ceiling in one pass.
  - The drum, bass and rhythm buses saturate at four times the sample rate, so far less of the curve's harmonics folds back into the band: under three drum buses the folded part went from 53–80 dB down to 100 dB down or lower.
- **How near an orchestra is heard** (`mix.c80_db`, with `mix.rules: 2`; [orchestration.md](skills/music-production/references/orchestration.md#how-near-the-orchestra-is-heard)). The skill's orchestral pieces were mixed far clearer than a hall is: the response such a mix gives its parts has a clarity (C80) of about +16 dB, where −2 to +2 dB is recommended for concert halls (Barron 2013). A piece now asks for a clarity and the hall's return moves to give it; the guide says which way to lean by the piece and where it is heard, since listeners themselves divide between an intimate sound and an enveloping one (Lokki et al. 2012). The clarity is worked out from the project file (each part's written level, the time its notes sound and its send) and from the spaces' impulse responses in the octaves of 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz, as the index is defined, so it is the same before a note is rendered and changes only with what is written; it compares the balance of early and late sound with a hall's and says nothing else of a hall. `analyze` reports the mix's C80 beside the range for halls.
- **A sound chosen by number.** `studio.py sounds --centroid 600-900 --attack 0.1-0.3` takes ranges of a patch's measured brightness and attack. The Surge XT catalog now measures every patch three times and keeps the medians, and calls a patch steady or moving by how far its brightness differed between the playings (1,144 of 2,997 moved by more than 10%). Each patch keeps the octave it is played at.
- **`analyze` reports the reverb**: `reverb` holds the spaces' early decay time and the mix's clarity, worked out from the project and the spaces' impulse responses ([measuring.md](skills/music-production/references/measuring.md)).

### Changed

- **The timing line follows how players keep to a beat** ([performance-and-mix.md](skills/music-production/references/performance-and-mix.md#player-like-deviation-musiclibexpression)). `expression.apply` now moves each part the way the research on keeping time describes it: by the next beat a player takes back a share of the distance from the beat, and a new small error comes on top (linear phase correction: Repp 2005). The share is 0.4, inside the range in which timing varies least (van der Steen and Keller 2013); a part's profile sets another with `correction`. Each onset also has a small delay of its own (Wing and Kristofferson 1973), and each part keeps to the score's beat by itself.
  - The size of the deviation is what it was, 2.5 ms on drums to 6 ms on a melody, and the loudness line is drawn as it was. On a steady sixteenth-note hi-hat the timing now has a standard deviation of 2.9 ms and a correlation of +0.64 between neighbouring notes, +0.45 a beat apart and +0.27 two beats apart.
  - A project file already written is not touched: its deviation is in its notes. A script run again writes another performance of the same size: other timing offsets and note lengths, and other values of the loudness line, which is drawn after them. On a sampled instrument a note near the border of two recorded dynamics can cross it, so an exposed line can sound different.
- **A drum hit takes one of its recorded takes by a draw of its own.** A drum part on a bank with several takes of a hit used to step through them in order; each hit now takes one by a draw made from the hit itself, the same on every render (`musiclib/variation.py`). A hit has the take of the hit before it on that drum and layer about one time in N, where the layer has N takes. This is the one place where a project made before 0.6 does not render sample for sample as it did: its drum parts play other takes of the same hits in the same dynamic layer, unless `performance.round_robin` is false. The velocities written in the project and in the exported MIDI are unchanged; the draw is made from the hit's id, its key and where it falls, so a hit that is moved draws again.
- **The four examples are made again** from their scripts with the new timing line: the same notes, harmony, sounds and mix in another performance. Among the notes of the parts that `expression.apply` shapes, the timing moved by a median of about 4 ms in absolute value (3.5 to 4.8 by piece) and the velocity by 2 to 3; parts written without it, such as the fughetta's harpsichord, did not change. They do not sound note for note as the recordings of examples-2 did. Their recordings are the release examples-3.
- **The horror style map is arranged by what each device does to a listener** and rests on more sources: instability and expectation kept waiting (Lehne and Koelsch 2015; Huron 2006), the signals of alarm (Arnal et al. 2015; Blumstein et al. 2010; Trevor et al. 2020) and the startle, beside the film-music writing it had.
- `tonal-references.json` gives its seven pieces' measures without links to recordings; the measures and `analyze --reference` are unchanged.
- **Three lists are shorter.** The J-pop map gives three chord progressions as examples and names the study that counts them (Uehara et al. 2016); the guide to character words gives three words and names the study that compares the collections (Moffat et al. 2022), and a few labels of each of the five clusters of mood labels where it listed all 29 (Hu and Downie 2007).
- **The style maps and genre guides are reworded** in about 180 rows and sentences; no trait, figure or citation changed. [sources-and-rights.md](skills/music-production/references/sources-and-rights.md) says what is taken into the skill and what is only cited, and [THIRD_PARTY.md](THIRD_PARTY.md) names Wikipedia and the two open textbooks among the works cited.

### Fixed

- The measuring guide and the note in `tonal-references.json` said that the reference pieces had passed a listening review. They are this skill's own pieces, measured; the text now says that and no more.
- The mixing guide described the study of engineers' mixes as eight engineers mixing the same eight songs. Each of the eight songs was mixed by eight engineers, in two classes of eight with four songs each (De Man et al. 2014).
- Corrections to what the guides say of their sources:
  - The 5 dB per octave of commercial recordings is read from the average spectrum of Pestana et al. (2013); the slopes that paper gives by genre are another measure.
  - The orchestral map names the authors of *Understanding Music* as its title page does (Clark, Heflin, Kluball and Kramer).
  - The organ guide gave a range of room volumes for Mapp's ten churches that the paper does not give; it is gone.
  - WCAG 1.4.7 is written for recorded audio that is mainly speech, and offers no background sound, a way to turn it off, or 20 dB under the speech; the scene-music guide had given only the last.
  - In Schutz et al. (2008) the 60% in minor is of the marimba pieces that are tonal.
  - The 10 to 12 dB of noise reduction and the limits a cleaned take is checked against are this skill's own, and the noise guide says so; ITU-R BS.1116-3 is cited for the method it sets out.
  - The timpani's tuned modes are given as Rossing (2001) gives them, the (2,1) mode a fifth and the (3,1) mode an octave above the principal tone, and the paper is cited in full.
  - A phrase answered by another instrument or group is an antiphonal contrast in McAdams, Goodchild and Soden's taxonomy, and one repeated in another colour a timbral echo; the chamber guide had called the first imitation.

Before release an independent review read this version's changes against 0.5.0. What it found in the new features was fixed before release and each fix is covered by a check: a bus that is a single click no longer stops the mix; the true peak of a signal with a part at half the sample rate is no longer read double; the delivery records the gain it applied; the clarity no longer depends on what the parts' audio holds; a clarity asked of a project that sends nothing to a reverb is refused; and the sound catalog keeps the licence 78 patches state for themselves. After that review, version 2 of the mix's rules was made again so that what it changes is set from the project file and not from a measurement of the piece's audio. A second review read that work; what it found was fixed with checks: a part played backwards counts in the clarity for the span it sounds, onsets nearer than a sixteenth note each have a delay of their own, and a wide mix no longer records a lead compressor it does not have. A third review read those fixes; what it found was fixed with checks: a beat written 1 or 1.0 is one onset and draws one delay, and one strum; and the time a part counts for in the clarity is the time its renderer holds its notes (a slur as one note, a mono line to the end of the note that owns the voice, a drum hit dropped by `key_map` not at all), where it had been the performed notes. It also had the texts say exactly what the code does where they said more: a lead written as a peak is left alone only while nothing after its level raises it; `analyze` needs the render's `project.json`; and a drum hit's take is drawn from its id, key and place together. A last review of those fixes found one more case of the same kind, fixed with a check: a Surge XT patch that is played an octave up counts for the keys it is played on, so two written pitches that land on one key at the top of the range are one key's notes in the clarity as they are in the render.

## [0.5.0] - 2026-11-10

Styles described by their traits, and work that survives a stop. 0.5.0 maps twelve families of styles in 88 entries, each trait with its source, and `review` takes its limits from the entry that governs each question; building blocks write drums from professional drummers' statistics and bass and accompaniment from named figures; `analyze` sets a master's spectral slope beside published genre means; Surge XT parts slide, take velocity on the filter and swell as the score asks; and every write is journaled, so a killed render is taken up by `--resume` and `clean` removes only what a record or the journal proves.

### Added

- **Building blocks** (`musiclib.blocks`): a drum groove, a bass line or an accompaniment written as notes to start from, from the piece's own settings ([building-blocks.md](skills/music-production/references/building-blocks.md)).
  - Drums follow Open Music Theory's three layers: the backbeat, the kick and the cymbals' pulse. What fills the layers in comes from professional drummers, feel by feel: twelve feels, from rock, soul, funk and hip-hop to Latin and the rock and jazz shuffles, measured from the [Groove MIDI Dataset](https://magenta.tensorflow.org/datasets/groove) (Gillick et al. 2019; Google LLC, CC BY 4.0). They give where each drum falls in the bar, how loud, how many notes a bar holds, how much the bars vary, and the fills with the crash after them. Complexity, intensity, fills and variation are quantiles of those drummers' distributions, and can differ per section, as Logic Pro's Drummer sets them per region.
  - Bass and accompaniment take the guides' figures (the two-feel, funk, bossa nova and walking lines, broken chords, comping, strummed chords) or a rhythm of the piece's own, written as Logic Pro's Session Player patterns mark main and probable hits. Pushes, an eighth early over a change; fills at phrase ends; a walking line through the scale tones of Open Music Theory's chord-scale table. Velocities follow MuseScore's table of dynamics. The comping answers the melody and the bass, leaving the eighths where they strike (Israels).
  - Writers given one tool's idea wrote stories more alike, and still did with five ideas to choose from (Doshi and Hauser 2024). To keep pieces from starting alike, no setting has a default, the seed comes from the piece's title, and the comping follows the piece's own melody. `blocks.candidates` sets several parts of one setting side by side, and the record names a choice that is its style's most common form. Measured at one setting across fifty pieces' seeds: fifty drum grooves; fifty rhythms from each comping figure but the steady broken chord; eight from the bass figures of fixed rhythm (root, two-feel, bossa nova, walking) through their fills and pushes over sixteen bars.
  - No number in the blocks stands in for what players do: each is the drummers', a source's or a tool's, a setting's definition, or a uniform choice by the seed ([building-blocks.md](skills/music-production/references/building-blocks.md#where-the-numbers-come-from)).
- **Style maps** ([style-maps.md](skills/music-production/references/style-maps.md)): what a style sounds like, described on seven axes (tempo, harmony, melody, rhythm, timbre, form, mix), family by family. Style names do not agree between catalogues (Pachet and Cazaly 2000: of three catalogues' genres, 70 words were common to all three), so an entry is described by its traits. A genre is a set of musical facts governed by norms, a sub-genre a subset, and a piece may lie in the intersection of genres (Fabbri 1981).
  - A project's `style` names an entry, or a primary entry with entries borrowed for some axes; each axis follows one entry, and each borrowing records its reason, as a creative combination is valued for an intelligible link (Boden 2004). `validate` refuses an entry the maps do not have.
  - `review` takes each check's limits from the entry governing the check's axis. Its record lists every limit with its origin: a source, or `ours` where the project set the number itself on its early pieces (11 of its 18 checks; the two added for brightness and tempo, below, take theirs from sources with margins of the project's own). The guides say which of the numbers they quote are the project's own. On projects without a `style`, review asks and records exactly what it did before (20 projects compared).
  - Twelve families, 88 entries: orchestral and classical (8), horror and suspense (4), electronic (16), J-pop and anime songs (5, fast electro J-pop among them), pop and ballads (5), rock and metal (14), beats (7), jazz, blues, funk and soul (9), Latin American, Caribbean and Iberian (9), Chinese (3), folk and roots (4), and the places of a role-playing game (4), whose town, overworld, dungeon and battle music differ in form and harmony (Elkins 2020) and take their sound from the game's own style. Each trait names its source, among them Music Theory Online's studies of dance music, post-rock and anime songs, the Weimar Jazz Database's measurements and corpus studies of J-pop; the 166 of 616 cells that no source read describes say so and give no advice. Each entry says what the skill plays it with and what the banks lack.
  - Tempos and section lengths measured on the [Harmonix Set](https://github.com/urinieto/harmonixset) (Nieto et al. 2019; annotations under the MIT licence, credited in THIRD_PARTY.md), 912 full tracks annotated by professional musicians: medians and shares by its genre labels for pop, hip-hop, rock and metal and the dance styles, and the tempo and form of disco, alternative and indie, country and reggaeton, which no source read described. In 422 pop songs, verses and choruses ran 8 bars in about half, 16 in about a fifth.
  - The tempo ranges `review` asks about say what they rest on, traced through Wikipedia's citations: Ableton's Learning Music lesson for house, techno (now 120–140), dubstep (now 135–145) and drum and bass; a production manual for trance; for the others a sentence citing a blog, a fan site or nothing.
  - Requests without an entry of their own are combined from entries: a J-pop ballad from the pop ballad with the anime opening's harmony. The anime underscore has no entry, as the sources read describe anime songs only; it is written as music for a scene.
  - Families and entries set limits only where their sources speak: the orchestral, electronic and beats families do not ask about pushes, rock does not ask about parallel fifths (power chords), the dance styles, the beats, funk, doom and a game's dungeon do not ask about a part's loop, and ambient and post-rock not about a static texture.
  - In the dance styles and the beats, `review` asks about every part repeating one block note for note only where no filter sweep, gain automation or controller curve moves the sound in that span (`processes_vary`): loops in dance music change by layers and continuous processes (Smith 2021; Garcia 2005).
- **Moods across styles** ([moods.md](skills/music-production/references/moods.md)): which cues carry happy, sad, peaceful and frightening music, from a study that varied mode, tempo, register, dynamics, articulation and timbre independently (Eerola, Friberg and Bresin 2013), with unease, elation at a peak and nostalgia from the maps. A mood moves the cues inside a style.
- **Brightness and air** ([performance-and-mix.md](skills/music-production/references/performance-and-mix.md#brightness-and-air)): what brightens a mix, by its sources: the genre's published top end, percussion (Elowsson and Friberg 2017), register and the louder recorded dynamics, the sound chosen, low-passes, the bands mixing books name (air at 10–20 or 12–20 kHz in most, as Moffat, De Man and Reiss 2022 list them), reverb, and what the chain itself does.
  - `analyze` measures Pestana et al. (2013)'s spectral slope, log magnitude on log frequency over 100 Hz–10 kHz, and sets it beside the mean of the US and UK number-one singles of the genre governing the piece's mix axis (`published_genre`): electronic −0.75, disco −0.80, hip-hop −0.81, pop −0.94, rock −0.98, soul −1.03, folk −1.18, jazz −1.29. It asks about a master more than 0.3 steeper, a margin this project chose. J-pop borrows pop's and says so; lo-fi, ambient and a game's places have none, with the reason.
  - `review` asks when most parts other than the bass are low-passed at or under 12 kHz: widespread low-pass filtering seldom helps and easily dulls a mix (Senior 2012). Lo-fi, unpolished on purpose, is not asked. The 0.4.0 agent pieces had low-passed 45 of their 163 parts there, eight pieces every part.
  - `review` asks when a piece's main tempo lies outside the range its style entry's source gives (twelve entries, from house's 115–130 BPM to smooth jazz's 90–105). In a closed range a half- or double-time reading counts as inside.
- **Character words** ([character-words.md](skills/music-production/references/character-words.md)): a quality a brief names (bright, warm, thick, spacious, bouncy, rousing, epic), grouped as research groups such words.
  - Sound: the three shared dimensions of timbre listeners used in two languages, luminance, texture and mass, with the adjectives loading on each and what was measured with them (Zacharakis, Pastiadis and Reiss 2014); the words people give to effects, the equalization that goes with warm and with thin, and the bands mixing books name, from air to fat (Moffat, De Man and Reiss 2022); the timbral attributes with models validated on listeners' ratings (Audio Commons, Pearce et al. 2018).
  - Motion: the cues measured to move listeners' judgements (articulation, tempo, syncopation, timing, register and dynamics). Feeling: perceived against felt emotion, AllMusic's mood labels in five clusters (Hu and Downie 2007), and the nine dimensions of music-evoked emotion of the Geneva Emotional Music Scale (Zentner et al. 2008, through Strauss et al. 2024).
  - No word is broken into cues unless a source does it: bouncy, epic or heroic have none in the sources read, and the guide says so.
- **Surge XT plays the 808 and the acid line as the score asks.** A Surge part takes `glide_ms`, one voice gliding only between overlapping notes over that many milliseconds without restarting its envelopes (Surge's mono single-trigger fingered portamento), and `velocity_to_cutoff`, velocity moving filter 1's cutoff in semitones; the patch is edited before it loads, read from Surge XT 1.3.4's sources, and a part without them sends it byte for byte. Where Surge is missing, the fallback keeps the slide.
  - The candidates were measured as played, with no listening ([surge-roles.json](skills/music-production/references/surge-roles.json)): two 808 patches slide in 82 ms at a setting of 80 with their notes within 8 cents; of thirteen acid patches, four take accents and slides, five accents only, four neither. A note that slides in keeps the velocity of the note the slide starts from (Surge XT's legato), so it takes no accent, and `review` names such notes. `review --config` names those patches and their settings for the built-in `bass_808` and `acid_bass`, which stay the fallback, and `audition` plays them with those settings (`surge:<patch>+glide_ms=80`).
  - The guides follow the sources on what the parts do (the TR-808's bass drum and its slides in trap and drill; acid house's resonance, accent and slide; Roland's TB-303 manual) and say that reading an accent as a velocity opening the filter is this skill's.
  - The electric pianos measured at four velocities: six grow brighter and louder, three brighter, three only louder ([sound-selection.md](skills/music-production/references/sound-selection.md)).
- **Risers, reverse cymbals and a recorded crash played backwards.** `surge.swell` sets the attacks of a Surge patch's amplitude and filter envelopes to the length of the part's notes; measured with it, a reverse crash and a dark noise riser peak at the note's end and rise by 25 to 62 dB, and four other riser patches peaked early or grew darker, so the built-in `noise_riser` stays the riser's default. A sampled part with `reverse: true` plays each note's recording backwards into the span the note sits in, so a kit's crash swells and stops on the downbeat, as tape reversal made it.
- **More building blocks** ([building-blocks.md](skills/music-production/references/building-blocks.md)): the traditional, hammer and bomb blast beats and the double kick (Wikipedia, Blast beat); a drum groove written on a step grid, voice by voice; the skank and the double skank (Wikipedia, Reggae), la pompe (Persinger 2014, *Premier Guitar*: the quick upstroke before beats 1 and 3) and the bossa nova batida (Wikipedia, Bossa nova) as accompaniment figures; `slides`, notes held into the next for a patch that glides; and `articulate`, each note's length a ratio of the time to the next onset at Eerola, Friberg and Bresin's levels.
- The selfcheck runs 148 checks (119 without a configuration); the new ones cover the building blocks, the style maps' limits, the published spectra, the low-passes, the tempo ranges, the Surge controls, the reversed notes, the journal killed after each of its lines and renames, a render killed and resumed, and cache entries cleaned by their own record.
- **`expression.apply(..., drift='time')`**: the player's slow timing and dynamics line wanders in time, on the sixteenth-note grid. A note added or removed changes only itself and the onset before it, so `compare` shows a revision where it is. Before, every note of a part moved when it gained one. The default, `drift='notes'`, keeps 0.4.0's line, so earlier scripts, the examples' included, write the projects they wrote. On a steady sixteenth-note part the two give the same statistics.

### Fixed

- **A killed render is taken up by `--resume`.** While a render works, its folder and each cache entry it writes keep a journal, `files.journal`, each line written whole: each new file's name before the file is made and again, with its file number, once it is made, and each file's size, time and number before it takes its name, as a database logs a change before it makes it (PostgreSQL, Write-Ahead Logging). A program's log is written the same way, under a new name, then given its own. A killed render so leaves only files its record or its journal proves: `--resume` removes the half-written and takes up the rest, and `clean` removes them all. A name alone proves nothing: the line before a file is made may be followed by a failure on a file someone else has there, and a file put later under a name noted made is another file, by its number. So a render killed in the instant between making a new file and noting it made leaves that file unproven, and so does a program the killed render had started when it makes its output anew after the render's own was removed: `--resume` names such a file and stops, and `clean` leaves it. Before, a render killed outright had a record of the stages it had finished only: `--resume` refused the files its last stage had begun, and `clean` left them. Measured: the example project's render killed at 32 moments spread over its journal lines and renames, cold cache and warm, each resumed to the master an unbroken render makes; a run of the write protocol killed after each of its lines and each rename left nothing its record or journal did not account for; renders of thirty random projects killed by the operating system at random moments found the MP3 and Ogg checks' decodes written outside the journal, now scratch files it names. ## [0.4.0] - 2026-11-09

Faster revisions with the same music. In the nine 0.3.0 agent sessions most of the time was the model's, and the agents opened the commands' JSON with code of their own 26 times a session, because the printed output did not answer what they had to decide. 0.4.0 changes what reaches the agent and what a render does, not how the music is made: the review's questions, the guidance and the audio are those of 0.3.0.

### Added

- **`studio.py round`**: one revision round in one call. It validates the project, reviews the score, renders a draft, analyzes it and compares it with the previous round (`--previous`), printing each step's summary as it finishes and writing `round.json` from the start, which names the step that stopped a round that failed; `--clip` adds the change old then new at one loudness, and `--json` prints round.json instead of the summaries. SKILL.md makes it the way to revise; the single commands remain for what a round does not do. Anthropic's guidance for agent tools: consolidate steps that are always chained.
- **Draft renders** (`render --draft`, and every round): the same audio, stems, buses and returns as a full render, without master.flac, the check that the MP3 decodes to the master's loudness, and a game loop's Ogg copies and layers, as REAPER's dry-run render processes everything without writing the output files. A draft's status is `DRAFT_PASS`; deliver a full render.
- **Frozen parts**: a part's cache entry keeps its processed stem, and the next render copies it while nothing it depends on has changed: its raw synthesis, its mix (not the sends and bus, which only the mixer reads), the song's length, the part a duck follows, the renderer's code, FFmpeg and NumPy. The copy is checked against its hash. A DAW plays a frozen track the same way. In our tests, moving one kick note in Sprinkle Satellite processed the kick and the six parts it ducks, and nothing else.
- **`studio.py clean`** lists the piece's render and round folders other than those kept (`--keep` the final render and the previous round), its auditions' caches and the cache entries the kept renders do not use, as Ableton Live's Cleanup does, and removes them only with `--remove`, as git clean deletes only with `-f`. It removes files, not folders: every folder a command writes records the files it wrote in `files.json` (path, size, modification time and file number), and clean removes a file only while that record lists it unchanged, as Ninja's clean tool removes only the outputs its build log names. A render, round or audition holds its folder while it runs (an operating-system lock, released however the run ends): clean leaves a folder at work alone, and a second run into it is refused at once, as Bazel serves one command at a time per output folder. A file put in such a folder, a file changed since or put back as a copy, another piece's render inside it, a folder copied elsewhere rather than moved and a folder from before 0.4.0 (no record) stay, and the report names them; a folder goes whole only when nothing else is left in it, its records last, so a clean stopped part-way is found again by the next. A cache entry goes only when it holds nothing but what renders write there, under the entry's lock. Links and junctions are never followed, on every Python the skill runs on. On the 27 GB folder of one 0.3.0 agent piece it would free 20 GB.
- **Short summaries**: `round`, `review`, `render`, `analyze`, `compare`, `audition` and `clean` print what an agent decides from, and end with the file and the keys holding the full figures; `--json` prints the whole report. The summaries hold every review question with the first places it occurs, the failed checks, the master against its target, each section's level, stereo and lead, the tonal balance against published measurements and a reference, and what changed. [outputs.md](skills/music-production/references/outputs.md) lists every key. On the nine agents' own outputs, the median summary is 1.9 k characters for a review (0.3.0 printed 1.1 k), 2.6 k for an analysis (5.5 k) and 3.2 k for a comparison (3.3 k), against 12 to 19 k for the full JSON.
- `report.json` records the seconds per stage and in FFmpeg by kind; each stem receipt, the part's synthesis and processing seconds and whether its stem was reused.

### Changed

- **Swing pairs are counted from each bar line**, as a drum machine delays the even-numbered steps of the beat (Linn 2013): every bar swings alike, and a bar holding an odd number of units (eighths in 5/8 or 7/8, quarters in 3/4) plays its last one straight. Before, pairs ran on from beat 0 across the bar lines, so in 7/8 every second downbeat played 80 ms late. Meters whose bars hold whole pairs play as before.
- `review` says what it counts as a push: an attack at most an eighth before a downbeat or half-bar that its part holds over or rests through (Longuet-Higgins and Lee 1984), in [development-and-groove.md](skills/music-production/references/development-and-groove.md).
- **`validate` finds a key struck twice at once**, or one tick apart, as written and as played, before anything renders. The error names both notes, the bar and beat and what to change; before, it came only at render time.
- **Renders are faster with the same audio.** The production delay's band, saturation, the mono low end and `compare`'s bands take their FFTs at a fast length. A stem's sample count can have a large prime factor, which made the delay the slowest step of a part. The K-weighting response is computed once per length and the master is measured once. Banks are hashed when a part first uses them, and again if the file changes. The mix's checks run while the master is made where the free memory holds both (measured: 15 float64 copies of the song together), and the MP3, FLAC and a game loop are made at the same time. On our test laptop, Sodium Lights (4:28, 14 parts) took 140 s from scratch with 0.3.0; with 0.4.0 a draft from an empty cache takes 80 s, and a full render that keeps every stem takes 52 s. Every stem not played by Surge XT matches 0.3.0's bit for bit, or within −277 dBFS for one delayed guitar, and the fughetta's master is bit for bit the same. On test signals the delay's band moved by at most −225 dBFS, saturation and the mono low end by at most −83 and −76 dBFS.
- **Guides in parts.**

### Fixed

Before release, 0.4.0 went through repeated independent code reviews and stress tests (random projects, excerpts and renders by the tens of thousands, hostile files, renders, rounds and `clean` at once on one cache) on Windows, macOS and Linux with Python 3.10 to 3.14. Each finding was reproduced, fixed and covered by a check. Listed here are the problems 0.3.0 had; those found in this release's own new features before release are not.

**Files and installs**

- **No command overwrites or writes through what is already there.** An output never takes an occupied path, a dangling link included, and is published only if nothing has taken the name meanwhile. Every file is written under a name of its own and swapped in whole, FFmpeg's and FluidSynth's outputs included, so a failed or stopped command leaves no half-written file, a hard link at the path keeps its bytes, and two runs never share a temporary name; on Windows the swap is tried again for up to a second when a virus scanner holds the new file. `soundbank.py cut` and `split` publish their notes and manifest together or not at all, `soundbank.py inspect` no longer overwrites its own input, and `build` refuses a receipt that names the bank, through a link or on a case-insensitive volume.
- **The installers keep what they did not put there.** `bootstrap.py` and `fetch_soundbanks.py` extract into a private folder and install file by file: a file at the target that the installer did not record there, a dependency receipt included, is set aside as `<name>.replaced-<time>`, never deleted, and so is a cached download that no longer matches the lock. A failed download leaves no partial file, and a configuration is written only where nothing has taken its path. `--delete-sources` deletes only files that are still the installer's own (an archive by its locked size and SHA-256, a sample by its git blob hash), never through a link or a junction, and names what it kept.
- **What an install fetches is pinned.** A bank built from a sample repository takes only the files the lock pins (each path with its git blob hash and size, `samples_sha256`): a listing that names other files, or a path that leaves the sample folder, is refused before anything is fetched, and each file is fetched no larger than its pinned size. requirements.txt names every file PyPI publishes for the pinned NumPy (2.1.2, and 2.5.4 on Python 3.14 and newer) by SHA-256, which puts pip in its hash-checking mode; install other packages with a pip command of their own. The pedalboard wheel comes from its locked address, no larger than its locked size and hashed as it arrives, and Surge XT plays only when the plugin that loads is the binary checked and pedalboard's files are those pip installed (each file's hash in the package's RECORD).
- `bootstrap.py` tries a download again after a dropped connection, a timeout, a server error (5xx or 429) or a download cut short, after 3, 6 and 12 s, as `fetch_soundbanks.py` does, and names the one that failed: one HTTP 504 from GitHub's servers stopped a whole install. A redirect that the host check refuses closes its connection.
- `selfcheck.py --work` takes only a new or empty folder.

**Files from elsewhere**

- Projects, reports, receipts, bank specs and manifests, and configurations are read through one reader that refuses a link, a file larger than its limit, JSON nested more than 64 levels (Python 3.14 parses far deeper than earlier versions) and a document of the wrong shape, naming the field. They were read through links, or stopped the command with a traceback.
- `validate` refuses a project that is not a JSON object and any field of the wrong type, naming it: a clock, a track or a `revision_history` of another type stopped the command, and a huge integer raised OverflowError. Before anything renders, it also refuses a synth patch's malformed `pwm` or `index_env`, an LFO given in both Hz and beats, notes that overlap as written in a part with slur legato (its one pitch wheel bent them together; crossfade legato plays them), a phaser rate outside FFmpeg's 0.1 to 2 Hz, and a part whose bank lacks its preset (FluidSynth put another preset in its place).
- A SoundFont whose preset or instrument tables do not hold together, as the SoundFont 2.04 specification and FluidSynth refuse them, or that is cut short inside a chunk, a game loop's chunks shorter than they count, a FLAC source without FLAC's `fLaC` marker (RFC 9639) and a WAV longer than a render can be (31 minutes) are refused: indices running back and forth made a 4 KB file expand into 8,192 zones. FFmpeg reads a FLAC source with its FLAC demuxer from local files only.
- `analyze` and `compare` validate a render folder's project and refuse one holding links, so no stem is read from wherever a link points; a render refuses an output given as a link.

**Rendering**

- **A Surge XT part plays on into the piece's tail**, as `tail_seconds` asks: its host stopped four seconds after the last note and the rest was silence, so a long release ended abruptly while the quiet-tail check passed. A release longer than the tail now fails that check, which says to lengthen the tail.
- **A render that fails or is stopped stops the programs it started**, by Ctrl+C or because a part failed: the parts still waiting do not start, those at work stop, and the render ends on the first failure and writes `report.json` with `status` FAIL, the stage it had finished, the part and the reason; `--previous`, `compare` and `analyze --reference` refuse such a render. A part whose synthesis is silent is named, with what to check.
- `--resume` refuses a folder holding links, a file the earlier render did not write, or one changed since (the user's own MIDI under a render's name).
- Delivered audio carries no tags from the files it was made from: every FFmpeg output is written with `-map_metadata -1 -map_chapters -1` (a tag added to a cached WAV reached master.wav, the MP3 and the FLAC).
- The additive synth's pitch bend rests at zero until its first point, and its bend and controllers follow the curve the MIDI holds, in beats, so the audio and the exported MIDI agree under a tempo change. A synth LFO, pulse width or arpeggio in beats follows the beats the voice plays, so a tempo change or rubato under a held note moves it as it moves the beat.
- A part's automation is applied in NumPy, sample by sample: an FFmpeg expression failed past 95 points and stepped the level every 4,096 samples (85 ms). Before its first point the part keeps the first point's level.
- A part's raw synthesis is found in the cache again only when no code it runs has changed (a vinyl noise part kept its old noise after `production.py` changed), and two renders of one piece at once no longer write one cache entry together: a part holds its entry across processes while it is made.
- Parts made at once share the memory free at the start, each holding what it was measured to need, instead of 2 GB each whatever the song; the built-in synth's filter takes a long note a chunk at a time (a two-minute pad: 3.7 GB, from 8.3 GB, the same samples), and a part that would need more memory than the machine has is refused before anything is synthesized.
- The mix reads its filtergraph from a file: on one command line, 64 parts in a deep folder passed the 32,767 characters a Windows command line holds.
- The scripts print a character their output cannot encode as an escape (`喆`) instead of stopping on it: on Windows, Python writes the output an agent reads through a pipe in the ANSI code page, which lacks characters some names hold. Folder and piece names may hold any characters; on Windows, setup.md asks for runtime and piece folders under 120 characters, as FluidSynth 2.6.1 does not opt in to paths over 260 characters.

**Game loops**

- Loop layers go through the mix's `mono_below_hz` and `wow_flutter`, as the full mix does: they kept the stereo lows the mix takes out, and their sum differed from the mix by 3 dB.
- `validate` refuses loop layers that split a bus which compresses or saturates its parts together (every bus but `fx`, unless `mix.dynamics` is `"wide"`), and says to keep those parts in one layer, give some another bus, or make the dynamics wide: each layer's bus acted on its own parts only, and the layers no longer added up to the mix.

**Excerpts and auditions**

- **An excerpt, and so an audition, plays a passage as the piece does.** Before, an excerpt could play a note the piece had already stopped, cut a note short, leave one out, or strike a key twice at once and fail to render.
  - A note is re-attacked at the cut only if it still sounds there, and not where its key is struck again within 100 ms as played (counting both notes' timing, the part's offset and swing); a legato overlap that only its gate carries past the cut is not re-attacked, and a note held over the cut that its gate or timing stopped is left out.
  - A note the piece plays across a cut plays the part of it the passage holds, at the timing and gate that put it on those ticks. Notes that a part's offset or timing plays before the cut start the excerpt; a note written in the next bar that they play early belongs to the next passage. Which notes the passage holds is decided on the tick grid the piece plays them on, after a change of tempo too.
  - A part held to one line (the additive lead, slur legato) stays one line in the piece's written order: the passage's own notes keep their places and lengths, and a note from before the cut ends sooner where the line leaves it no room. In other parts, a pickup that the passage strikes again on the cut is written a few ticks later, its timing keeping where it plays.
  - A linear CC ramp keeps its value at the cut and the end, the piece's ritardandi that reach into the passage are kept (`clock.rubato.spans`), a rhythmic gate keeps the piece's phase (`mix.gate.phase_beats`), and an older free-form harmony object is kept as it is.
  - Left as it is: a part offset beyond timing's ±100 ms starts a note from before the cut late, or leaves it out.
- An audition that renders a version again with a longer tail removes only its render's own files, and stops if anything else is there: it removed the whole folder. `audition --solo` plays a part ducked to another part, leaving the duck out and saying so, instead of failing every version, and an audition records what stopped it.

**Review, analysis and compare**

- `review` of a dense score no longer takes quadratic time: minor seconds and ninths and low intervals are found through an index of the notes by pitch (2,000 dense notes: 0.05 s, from 0.27 s), with the same findings on random projects. It checks the text it reads (a brief, decisions, intents, roles, note labels), and a spreadsheet formula in a note's phrase or articulation is written to notes.csv with a leading apostrophe (OWASP, CSV Injection).
- `analyze` gives no lead figures for a section where the lead rests, rather than NaN, which stopped it. It refuses two regions of one name (a `--span` label given twice, or one named as a section is): their figures were mixed, and a section's came out 19.6 dB wrong with a cut suggested on them. `round` asks before it renders.
- `compare` gives no overall level for a file that changed throughout (a rewritten harp showed −332 dB): where one gain cannot match the two files, `level_db` is null and the summary says so.
- The records meant to be shared (the comparisons, audition.json) name files relative to their folders, and a Surge fallback's reason names files, not paths; the logs keep the full paths ([outputs.md](skills/music-production/references/outputs.md) says what stays local).

**Sound banks**

- A bank build loads a take once for each way its layers cut and measure it, and its take cache keeps the measured pitch, so two layers that measure one take differently each keep their own partials. `soundbank.py cut` keeps no note of a run in which a note fails its hash, and says why. `configure` checks its SoundFont's tables, as `add-soundfont` does. The crossfade legato planner finds each next note by bisection, in N log N rather than N squared.

- **`musiclib.voicing.voice_lead`** voices a chord progression for a keyboard, pad, string or brass part, chosen across the whole progression so the voices move as little as possible (common tones held).

  - `against`: the pitches other parts sound with each chord (the melody, the bass, the left hand). The voicing keeps minor seconds and ninths away from them and, where it can, leaves their pitches to them.

### Known issues

- Nobody but a person can say whether a piece works. The agent cannot listen; `review` and `analyze` only locate problems.
- Pitch bends move the whole sample, so a sampled guitar bend lacks a real string's change of tone, and fast runs cannot be slurred.
- The free banks have limits:
  - the YR basses have one velocity layer and sample only up to B♭2;
  - the VCSL piano has three velocity layers and no pedal-down samples (the Osiris piano has both pedal states, in two layers);
  - the cello's takes carry no vibrato of their own;
  - the double bass's vibrato takes repeat the player's bow changes about every 2.3 s, and the piccolo is sampled on five notes;
  - the section strings, the clarinet and the tremolo speak slowly;
  - marimba and xylophone are sampled sparsely;
  - the trumpet's vibrato samples last about 4.7 s.
- The erhu's sustained takes last 2–3 s, so a longer held note repeats its last second; the VCSL tenor saxophone's two channels are partly out of phase and want `mix.width` near 0.2; the steel drum's top notes keep their recorded tuning.
- The synthesizers run in NumPy, a few times slower than real time for dense parts.
- The timpani's tuning was measured from the recordings, not by ear; an independent by-ear mapping of some of the same takes sits about half a semitone lower.
- Layer loops carry no compressor or limiter, so a game playing them together needs a limiter on its music bus.
- Linux and macOS are tested in CI with the system tools only; samples can differ slightly from the pinned Windows builds.
- `declick` is for takes recorded one note per file: it reads clicks on the average of the channels, so a click recorded in opposite polarity on the two can be missed, and it cannot tell a second strike's hammer noise from a knock, which on a take holding more than one note would lower the new note's first frames.
- `match_notes: "across"` measures each dynamic against its own median, so a dynamic recorded over less of the range than the others can step slightly at the edge of its range (on the Kawai, 0.5 dB at most).
- Installing banks assumes a runtime folder that no other program changes during the install: archive members are checked against links in the destination before they are written, not while.
