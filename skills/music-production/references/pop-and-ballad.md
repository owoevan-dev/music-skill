# Pop and ballads: an instrumental lead that sings

Read this when the request is any of these:
- instrumental pop;
- a pop ballad, a power ballad or a piano ballad;
- an emotional pop cue for a video or a game;
- a song-like piece where an instrument carries the tune.

Its sub-styles (up-tempo pop, the ballad, disco, synth-pop, hyperpop), each on the seven axes with its sources, are in [style-map-pop.md](style-map-pop.md).

This guide covers two things: a lead instrument that phrases like a voice, and a song form that builds to its chorus. Each piece of guidance names its source; the layouts and tables are example plans built from them, not norms.

## Brief

Settle these from the request, deciding what it leaves open and saying so:

- **Pop or ballad.** Up-tempo pop or a slow ballad, and whether the ballad becomes a power ballad when the band enters.
- **The lead.** Which instrument sings, and whether it changes between sections, for example piano in the verse and cello in the chorus (Instruments below).
- **Length.** The cue's or the song's length.
- **Ending.** A final chord or a short tag, or a fade.

## Form

- **Get to the hook early.** Léveillé Gauvin (2018) measured 303 US top-10 singles from 1986 to 2015. Their instrumental intros shrank from about 20 seconds in the mid-1980s to about 5 seconds. Start with the hook, or keep the intro short.
- **Verse, pre-chorus, chorus** (Open Music Theory, Verse-Chorus Form):
  - Verses tend to begin on the tonic.
  - The pre-chorus gains energy toward the chorus through four devices: shorter units, chords that change faster, harmony that leaves the tonic, and an ending left open (Summach 2011). [anime-opening.md](anime-opening.md) lists them with the J-pop build.
  - The chorus is more intense than the verse: a fuller or busier texture, a higher melody, or both (Summach 2012). Choruses most often begin on the tonic.
- **Bridge.** In verse-chorus form a bridge appears once, if at all, with the last chorus after it. It replaces the verse or pre-chorus of its cycle (Open Music Theory).
- **Final chorus.** Bigger than the first: keep some layers back for it, such as strings, a countermelody or a sub-bass layer, so the song has somewhere to build (Senior 2024).

An example layout for an instrumental ballad at 72 BPM, where a 4/4 bar lasts 3.33 s: 29 to 34 bars, about 97 to 113 seconds before the last chord rings out. For 90 seconds (27 bars), make the intro and the turn one bar each, the final chorus four and the tag one.

| Section | Bars | Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Intro | 2 | the hook's first phrase, piano alone |
| Verse | 8 | the lead; piano; the bass from bar 5 |
| Pre-chorus | 4 | strings enter; the harmony leaves the tonic; a fill |
| Chorus | 8 | the hook, higher; the drums enter; strings sustain |
| Turn | 2 | a breath: piano and strings; the new key's dominant |
| Final chorus | 4–8 | up a step: the hook doubled in octaves; the full band |
| Tag | 1–2 | the hook's last phrase, ringing |

## Harmony

- **Loops.** Four chords, I, IV, V and vi, probably turn up in pop more than any others. Open Music Theory names the four-chord schemas they form, each with its own sound:
  - **doo-wop:** I–vi–IV–V, common in 1960s pop; ii can replace IV;
  - **singer/songwriter:** I–V–vi–IV or vi–IV–I–V, common from the mid-1990s. Without an authentic cadence it can leave the tonic ambiguous between the major key and its relative minor;
  - **hopscotch:** IV–V–vi–I, common since 2010.

  The royal road, IV–V–iii–vi, is in [anime-opening.md](anime-opening.md).
- **Plagal and Mixolydian colour.** In a corpus of 100 rock songs, IV was the most common chord after I, especially right before the tonic, and ♭VII was the most common root outside the major scale (de Clercq and Temperley 2011). So IV–I and ♭VII–I sound like pop and rock more than every cadence on V–I ([rock-and-metal.md](rock-and-metal.md)).
- **The key change** (Dalla Riva 2022, 2025):
  - **The lift.** Moving the key up a half or a whole step late in the song was the most common key change for decades: 52% of the key changes in US number-one hits from 1958 to 1990.
  - **Then and now.** From 1960 to 1995, 20–35% of each year's number ones changed key. From 2010 to 2020 only one did, and a few have returned since.
  - **So** treat the last-chorus lift as a choice, not a default.

## Melody for the lead

- **Range.** Perricone (2000) asks of a vocal line whether its range is too great, and whether the range within a section changes too quickly. A higher melody is one of the ways a chorus can intensify (Summach 2012, above).
- **Phrases with breaths.**
  - In pop and rock a phrase typically lasts four bars, one line of the lyric (Open Music Theory, Melody and Phrasing).
  - A phrase ends by resting, holding or resolving, where a singer would breathe (Perricone 2000).
  - A lead that takes a voice's place needs the singer's breaths.
- **The hook and the section.** Pop and rock sections usually have two, three or four phrases: aa′, aa′b or srdc (Open Music Theory). In srdc an idea is stated and restated, departs, and concludes ([climax-and-melody.md](climax-and-melody.md)).
- **Syncopation.** Rock and pop melodies anticipate strong beats, for example a note on the "and" of four tied over the bar line (Tan, Lustig and Temperley 2019). Medium syncopation moves listeners most (Witek et al. 2014).
- **Phrasing on samples.** A sampled lead re-attacks every note unless told otherwise. Use `performance.legato`:
  - crossfade on bowed and wind leads that have recorded dynamics;
  - slur on the others.

  Also use a dynamics curve or CC1 for the cello's vibrato, and bends on guitar ([performance-and-mix.md](performance-and-mix.md)).

## Rhythm and drums

- **The beat** (Open Music Theory, Drumbeats):
  - Most pop drumbeats keep a backbeat: the snare accents beats 2 and 4.
  - Common beats besides the basic rock beat are double-time, half-time and four-on-the-floor.
- **The arrival.** Reserving the drums for a later section gives the song room to build (Senior 2024). A drum pattern usually changes from section to section, and a small fill at the end of an eight-bar section leads into the next (Dow 2010).
- **Tight drums and ghost notes.** Small timing deviations did not make a simple pattern groove more (Frühauf, Kopiez and Platz 2013). Keep the kit tight. Ghost notes are quiet hits between the main ones, and velocities rise toward the end of a bar or a verse (Dow 2010).

## Arrangement

An example plan, built from the points above: build by addition from verse to chorus (McAdams, Goodchild and Soden 2022 describe such gradual addition), and hold layers back so the last chorus has somewhere to go (Senior 2024).

| Part | Verse | Pre-chorus | Chorus | Final chorus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Piano | the lead, or chords in the middle register | broken chords rising | chords on the beat, octaves in the bass | the hook in octaves, or high chords |
| Bass | none, or roots from the second half | roots with passing notes | roots on the beat, octaves | the same, louder |
| Drums | none, or a rimshot and hats | a fill into the chorus | full kit, crash on the downbeat | the same; a cymbal swell into it |
| Strings | none | sustained, entering low | sustained chords; a countermelody | the hook an octave up, or a high countermelody |
| Lead | its first register | climbing | higher, the hook | the hook, doubled |

## The mix

- **The lead on top.** Read `lead_presence` in `analyze`. The finished pieces' values in [measuring.md](measuring.md) are data points, not targets.
- **Brightness.** It comes from the percussion and the upper octaves (Elowsson and Friberg 2017). A ballad without drums is darker in `analyze`, and that is expected. Measured here, the recording and the touch change a piano's brightness more than EQ does ([performance-and-mix.md](performance-and-mix.md), A bright pop piano).
- **Width and centre.** The lead, the bass, the kick and the snare are conventionally centred, and other parts spread for width (Flint 2005).
- **Loudness.** Spotify plays tracks at −14 LUFS ([Spotify for Artists](https://support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/loudness-normalization/), Loudness normalization); deliver at `target_lufs` −14 with the bus chain.

## Symptoms and causes

| Symptom | Likely causes | What to check or change |
|---|---|---|
| The chorus does not lift | the verse as dense and as high as the chorus; no build | a chorus denser or higher than the verse (Summach 2012); the pre-chorus devices; layers held back (Senior 2024) |
| The lead sounds like a keyboard demo | re-attacked notes; constant velocity; no breaths | legato, a dynamics curve, phrase-end rests (Perricone 2000), vibrato or bends |
| Boring after the first chorus | the second half repeats the first | a bridge, which replaces a verse or pre-chorus (Open Music Theory); a final chorus with new layers or a key change |
| Muddy | piano left hand, bass and cellos close together in a low octave | wide spacing low (Open Music Theory, Jazz Voicings); one owner of the low end; a pad's energy below about 200 Hz removed (Senior 2010) |
| The lead is buried | pads and strings in the lead's register | chords kept below the lead (Israels, in [anime-opening.md](anime-opening.md)); mute parts one at a time to find what masks it (Senior 2018); `lead_presence` |
