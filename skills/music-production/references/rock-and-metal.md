# Rock and metal: riffs, power chords and a band that hits together

Read this when the request is any of these:
- rock: classic, hard, alternative, indie, punk or pop-punk;
- metal: heavy, thrash, power, melodic, metalcore, or a djent-like groove;
- a guitar-driven cue for a game, a trailer or a video;
- an instrumental for a rock band.

Its sub-styles, classic rock to nu metal, each on the seven axes with its sources and what `review` asks there, are in [style-map-rock.md](style-map-rock.md).

## Brief

Settle these from the request; what it leaves open is decided after the piece's words are drawn (SKILL.md, steps 1 and 2), and said so:

- **Style.** The traits below are from the Wikipedia articles on each style and the sources they cite (Sources); take the tempo from the brief and a reference of the style. What the guitars play, from the studies read, is in [Writing the guitar part](#writing-the-guitar-part):

  | Style | Drums | Guitars | Lowest string |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Classic and hard rock | backbeat; eighths on the hi-hat or ride | power chords and open chords; minor-pentatonic riffs; a lead with bends | E2 |
  | Alternative, indie | backbeat; toms in the verse | clean arpeggios in the verse, distorted chords in the chorus | E2 |
  | Punk, pop-punk | driving eighths; the snare on every beat for a lift | eighth-note power chords, downstrokes | E2 or E♭2 |
  | Traditional heavy metal (slow in the early style, faster from the late 1970s) | a rock backbeat; eighths on the hi-hat or ride; the kick doubling the gallop; the double kick in places, not throughout | Aeolian and Phrygian riffs, palm-muted and open power chords, pedal points; the gallop; twin leads in thirds | E2 or E♭2; the early style tuned down as far as C♯2 |
  | Thrash metal | the thrash beat (kick and snare alternating on eighths, the ride on every eighth); bursts of double kick | fast, palm-muted riffs on the low string with quick chord changes; chromatic steps, tritones and diminished intervals | E2, or D2 in drop D |
  | Power and melodic metal | power metal: a constant stream of sixteenths on the double kick, or thrash-style bursts; melodic death metal: fast double kick and occasional blast beats | power metal: rapid streams of notes over chords that change once a bar or slower, keyboards or an orchestra, fast solos; melodic death metal: harmonised guitar riffs as the melody | E2 or lower |
  | Metalcore (breakdowns in half time) | double kick in the heavy parts; in breakdowns a half-time snare, the kick in unison with the chugs, the china | low-tuned, percussive open-string chugs; a melodic chorus; often no guitar solo | drop D down to A (the banks stop at B1) |

- **Tuning.** The distorted guitar banks reach down to B1 (MIDI 35). So standard tuning (E2, MIDI 40), drop D (D2, 38), drop C (C2, 36) and a seven-string's low B (B1, 35) all play on recorded samples; nothing lower does. The bass plays an octave below: D1 is MIDI 26.
  - How low metal's guitars were tuned, in ten isolated guitar tracks for each decade (Herbst 2017a): tuning down was uncommon until death metal spread in the 1990s. The 1980s tracks: six in standard tuning, three a semitone down, one two. The 1990s thrash tracks: seven standard, three down one to three semitones. The 2000s death metal tracks: three to five semitones down, most around C or B. The 2010s: between B and A♭, and the bass goes down with the guitars.
- **Weight.** A clean or a distorted verse; one distorted guitar or a wall of four.
- **The lead.** A lead guitar, or another instrument in the singer's place ([anime-opening.md](anime-opening.md)).
- **Ending.** One of:
  - a last hit, cut short;
  - a held chord under cymbals and a drum roll, closed by one more hit;
  - a fade.

## Form

- **Verse, chorus, solo.** The usual frame is verse–chorus form. A pre-chorus builds toward the chorus (Summach 2011), and a guitar solo often takes the bridge's place, over the verse's chords or new ones.
- **The rotation and its bridge.** Since the mid-1960s the default of rock and pop is a rotation of verse and chorus twice, a contrasting bridge, and the rotation again, often shortened or altered (Covach 2005 and Temperley 2018, in Hudson 2021). Hudson (2021) counted the 195 tracks of 20 albums often named among heavy metal's best, a list that leans to the 1980s:
  - 59.5% have exactly that form, and 87.2% a rotation with a bridge somewhere (the rotation three times, or no return after the bridge);
  - in 46.7% a module, often the verse's riff alone, comes before each verse's voice;
  - bridges are often longer than rock's, beyond a minute and of several sections;
  - in 84 of the 148 songs whose rotation returns, the intro's material leads back into it;
  - in thrash, intensity builds through each verse to its chorus (Pillsbury 2006, in Hudson 2021).

  In an instrumental the lead takes the voice's entries.
- **The riff.** A short figure, repeated. In an instrumental it can carry the hook; plan where it and the lead melody take turns in front.
- **Quiet and loud.** A clean, thin verse against a distorted chorus: the "loud-quiet-loud" dynamic, a staple of alternative rock since the late 1980s (Wikipedia, in Sources).
- **Metal sections.** The **breakdown** is a slower or half-time passage of chugs in unison with the kick. In twenty-first-century metal, metalcore especially, it works as a section of its own, like a verse or a chorus (Gamble 2019).
- **Odd groupings over a steady pulse.** Pieslak (2007) explains one progressive-metal band's rhythm through odd time signatures, mixed meter and superimposition. To write a superimposed riff, let the guitars and kick cycle in groups of 5 or 7 sixteenths while the hi-hat and snare keep 4/4, until the two meet again at a phrase end.

## Harmony and riffs

- **IV and ♭VII.** De Clercq and Temperley (2011) analysed 100 songs from Rolling Stone's 500 greatest.
  - IV was the most common chord after I, especially right before the tonic.
  - ♭VII was the most common root outside the major scale.

  So IV–I and ♭VII–IV–I sound more like rock than a cadence on V–I does.
- **Modal and pentatonic patterns.** Biamonte (2010) describes the patterns of classic rock:
  - the double-plagal ♭VII–IV–I;
  - the Aeolian ♭VI–♭VII–i;
  - chord patterns built on the pentatonic scale.
- **Melody against the chords.** Temperley (2007) found that rock melodies often leave non-chord tones unresolved. This happens mostly in pentatonic melodies and in verses; choruses more often bring melody and chords together. So a pentatonic lead can sit over the verse's chords without following them, then lock to the chorus's.
- **Power chords under distortion.**
  - Distortion and harmonic complexity both reduce sensory consonance, most of all together (Herbst 2018).
  - With an overdriven or distorted tone, listeners rated power chords and major triads as clearly more pleasant than minor triads and altered dominants. Styles with more distortion, such as metal, rely more on power chords (Herbst 2019).
  - Distortion's harmonics and intermodulation give most chords a major quality, the power chord included: a distorted major triad sounds much like a power chord, while a minor triad's third clashes with that implied major third and turns rough. Distorted minor triads are rare in classic heavy metal (Lilja 2015).

  So:
  - distorted rhythm guitars play root and fifth, with or without the octave, or at most a major triad;
  - minor thirds, sevenths and added notes go to the bass, the lead, keys, strings or a clean guitar, and `review` still checks the written chord list;
  - clean guitars can play full chords and added notes.
- **Dark colours in metal.** Heavy metal riffs rest on three harmonic traits: modal progressions, tritone and chromatic motion, and pedal points.
  - Traditional heavy metal favours the Aeolian and Phrygian modes: Aeolian i–♭VI–♭VII and i–♭VII–♭VI, and the Phrygian i–♭II–i (E–F–E), alongside pentatonic and blues-derived figures.
  - Thrash leans on chromatic steps, the tritone (E–B♭) and diminished intervals rather than one scale.
- **Pedal-point riffs.** Alternate the open low string (the tonic) with fretted notes above it. The low note holds the key while the upper notes move.

## Rhythm and drums

- **The backbeat.**
  - The snare on 2 and 4; the kick is often syncopated; the cymbals keep a regular pulse (Open Music Theory, Drumbeats).
  - Patterns change from section to section, for example from hi-hat in the verse to ride in the chorus, and a small fill at the end of a section leads into the next (Dow 2010).
- **Tight, not loose.** Small timing deviations did not make a simple drum pattern groove better (Frühauf, Kopiez and Platz 2013). `musiclib.expression` keeps the drums' deviation small; keep the rhythm guitars' `feel` within a few milliseconds of the drums.
- **Punk** (Pearson 2019).
  - The punk of 1977: a fast rock beat, usually under 200 BPM, the snare on 2 and 4 and the kick centred on 1 and 3 with variations; time on the hi-hat's edge once or twice a beat, crash or ride accents where the riff arrives. Tracks of two or three minutes in verse–chorus form.
  - Hardcore, from the early 1980s: kick and snare alternate 300 to 400 times a minute and more. The backbeat is then no longer felt as beats 2 and 4, cymbal and kick accents outline larger patterns that match the riff, and the riff becomes the unit of metre; groupings of 3+3+2 against even ones (the D-beat).
  - Basic guitar breaks rather than long solos, and heavy, dry drums (Wikipedia, Punk rock).
- **Metal figures**, each tied to its style (the Wikipedia articles in Sources):
  - **traditional heavy metal: the gallop**, an eighth and two sixteenths, in the guitars and the bass, often doubled by the kick, over a rock backbeat; the double kick in places, not throughout;
  - **thrash: the thrash beat**, also called the skank beat: a sped-up 2/4 rock or polka beat, kick and snare alternating on eighths with the ride on every eighth; bursts of double kick;
  - **power and melodic metal: the double kick**, a constant stream of sixteenths under the snare's accents, or bursts of three to six beats in the thrash manner;
  - **metalcore: the half-time breakdown**, the snare on beat 3 of each bar, the kick in unison with the chugs, and the china or a crash on the accents;
  - **death and black metal: the blast beat**, from hardcore punk and grindcore: a very fast figure shared among kick, snare and a cymbal. The traditional blast alternates snare and kick in a single-stroke roll, the ride with the kick; the hammer blast plays kick and snare together on eighths. This guide does not cover these styles further.
- **On the building blocks.** `blocks.drums` writes the thrash beat, the double kick and the traditional, hammer and bomb blasts from its layers, and anything else on its step grid ([building-blocks.md](building-blocks.md), Drums).
- **Velocities.** The `rusty` kit has up to 14 velocity layers and 4 round robins. Drummers hit harder with the stronger hand, so alternate hits differ in velocity (Dow 2010):
  - alternate two values a few steps apart, for example 112 and 104;
  - accent the beat.

## Writing the guitar part

What a rhythm guitar plays is a choice among several ways of playing. The sources below describe the repertoires they studied, none a whole style's rule, and a part that keeps to one of them throughout is one choice among many. Each entry says what the way of playing is, where its source finds it, and how it is written for the direct guitar played by string ([below](#the-direct-recording-through-an-amplifier-mixamp)).

- **Riffs in modules.** A riff is repeated to make a module: often 2, 4 or 8 times in metal (Pillsbury 2006, in Hudson 2021), most typically four in the early American hardcore punk Easley (2015) studied, four bands' recordings of 1978 to 1983. A new module begins where the riff, the texture or the instrumentation changes. Guitars and bass usually play a metal riff in unison (Hudson 2021).
- **A riff's two parts.** In those recordings a riff tends to be a statement and a contrasting close, and Easley (2015) finds four ways the repetition inside one is laid out:
  - a gesture two or three times, then a contrasting one;
  - a statement, then a new gesture twice;
  - a statement, then the same begun again, from the same pitch and place, and altered;
  - a gesture, then the same transposed: the hand's shape moved along the neck, most often upward.
- **Power chords.** Root, fifth and optional octave: one shape of the fretting hand, slid along the neck and moved from string to string (Easley 2015). In the punk of 1977 they act more as timbre and line than as harmony (Pearson 2019). Written, in standard tuning: `part.shape(bar, beat, [(6, f), (5, f + 2), (4, f + 2)], beats)`, the same offsets from string 5, at any fret `f` that leaves `f + 2` on the neck (24 frets). A string tuned down moves its own frets: in drop D the shape from string 6 is one fret across, `[(6, f), (5, f), (4, f)]`.
- **What the riff does, by the drums' time feel.** A time feel keeps the tempo and changes how often kick and snare alternate: normal time, half time, double time, and faster. Garza (2021) finds six kinds of riff in metal since 2000, a list he calls not exhaustive, each at home in some feels. The table is a summary of his six types in this guide's words:

  | Riff | What the guitars play | Feel, and where he finds it |
  |---|---|---|
  | Straight, open divisions | a steady stream of the beat's divisions, not palm-muted; accompaniment | normal time; often choruses, where it leaves the listener the melody |
  | Pedal tone | a repeated palm-muted note, typically the open low string, with fretted notes played open above it (single notes, power chords or full chords), as long as the pedal's notes or longer | normal or double time; all metal, thrash and death above all |
  | Breakdown | low notes in uneven rhythms, in unison with the bass and sometimes the kick, staccato with silence between the strokes | half time; metalcore and deathcore above all |
  | Long durations | notes longer than a beat | slow feels; doom |
  | Weak-beat syncopation | rests or ties where weak beats would be struck; played open, on the kick's and the snare's rhythm | double time; black metal, metalcore, grindcore |
  | Tremolo | constant double picking at very fast tempos, single notes or chords | faster than double time (the blast beat); death, thrash, black above all |

  Palm muting strengthens the low bass and reduces the middle and treble (Herbst 2017a, measured). Written: the pedal's notes on the open string with `"mute": "palm"`, the notes above it without; a breakdown's strokes with `"mute": "palm"`, or as short open notes with the rests written between them; tremolo as repeated sixteenths or thirty-seconds on one string. Bassists in these riffs tend to play open notes rather than palm-muted ones (Garza 2021).
- **Sixteenth-note strumming with scratches** (in Japanese teaching, cutting). The strumming hand moves down and up on every sixteenth without stopping, and the fretting hand decides what each stroke is: pressed, the chord sounds; released until the fingers only rest on the strings, the stroke is a percussive scratch (Bowman 2023).
  - Chord stabs are kept short. Moving a stab among the four sixteenths of a beat moves the accent; a beat may hold one, two or three stabs, and the beats of a bar may differ: "there is no right or wrong, and most players tend to lean toward a few favorite combinations" (Bowman 2023).
  - His examples use a chord's upper strings, and a whole groove can stay on one chord. Striking the muted lower strings along with the sounding upper ones adds percussive weight (Fujioka 2021).
  - Written: every sounding stroke a `shape` of short notes on the upper strings, every scratch the same strings with `"mute": "dead"`; `musiclib.expression` with `{"strum": 4, "strum_grid": 0.25}` moves the hand down and up on the sixteenths ([performance-and-mix.md](performance-and-mix.md)).
- **Octaves.** The same note on two strings an octave apart thickens a line without adding to the harmony (Clay 2013). The string between the two is kept quiet: the fretting hand's first finger rests against it while the pick strums across all three. A phrase in octaves moves the hand along the neck a great deal. Clay shows them as rhythmic punches with the band and as the answer to a phrase first played in single notes.
  - Written, in standard tuning: `part.shape(bar, beat, [(5, f), (3, f + 2)], beats)`; the same offsets from string 6, and from strings 4 and 3 the upper note three frets up instead of two. The damped string between them gives a click under the strum: add it on the string next below the root's, at the root's fret (`part.fret(bar, beat, 4, f, beats, mute='dead')` for the shape from string 5), or leave it out for two strings sounded alone. In drop D the sixth string's octave on string 4 lies on the same fret: `[(6, f), (4, f)]`.
- **Chords left to ring, note by note.** No source read. In the skill: each note of the chord on its own string, written as long as it should ring; a string's note stops when that string is struck again and the others ring on. `clean` or `edge`, with the part's effects after the amplifier (below).
- **The lead.** Bends and vibrato: [performance-and-mix.md](performance-and-mix.md) (Grimes 2014). `performance.bend` acts on the whole part, so a bent note is written while no other string of the part rings. A lead whose notes are joined with `performance.legato` is written without `strings`, since a part played by string takes no legato; a lead played by string strikes every note, and its bends and slides do the joining.
- **An intro that builds.** Almost every song of Hudson's (2021) corpus begins by building the riff up an instrument at a time, a way of starting found across popular music (Attas 2015, in Hudson 2021): one guitar plays the riff alone, and after one or two repetitions the others enter, together or one by one, often first only on accents. The first guitar may stand hard to one side, the band's entry filling the image (Smialek 2016, in Hudson 2021); on recordings since the 1970s, intros and breaks leave one side's guitar alone, and the other side's entry heightens the wall of sound (Herbst 2017a).

## Guitars on the banks

### The direct recording through an amplifier (`mix.amp`)

`fsbs_direct` is the same guitar as the three banks below before any amplifier: the signal of its pickup, 120 takes in one channel, B1–D6. It is not meant to be heard as it is. `mix.amp` is its amplifier and loudspeaker:

```json
{"id": "gtr_l", "role": "rhythm", "engine": "soundfont", "soundfont": "fsbs_direct", "patch": {"bank": 0, "program": 0},
 "mix": {"level_dbfs": -22, "pan": -0.8, "amp": {"voice": "crunch", "drive": 0.6}}}
```

- **Why.** All the part's notes pass one amplifier together, so a chord's notes act on one another as they do in a real one (the sum and difference tones that give a distorted power chord its weight: Harmony and riffs, above). A recording that was distorted note by note cannot do that.
- **Voices.** `clean` (full chords, arpeggios), `edge` (breaks up when struck hard), `crunch` (rhythm), `lead` (more gain, a forward middle), `high_gain` (tight low end, for palm-muted riffs and metal). `drive` 0–1 sets how hard the first stage is driven. The chain has the structure the literature gives for a guitar amplifier (gain stages with filters between them, a tone filter, a power stage and a filter for the loudspeaker: Pakarinen and Yeh 2009), run at eight times the sample rate, as digital distortions usually are (Yeh, Abel and Smith 2007). The voices are this skill's own: their numbers are choices, not measurements of any amplifier, and are starting points to be changed by ear.
- **Dynamics are the drive.** The amplifier's input follows the velocities: a softer stroke is cleaner as well as quieter. Write accents and ghosted strokes with velocity; on `edge` and `crunch` that alone moves a part between clean and broken up. The bank plays one of four takes of a note by the velocity's exact value, so the small differences `musiclib.expression` gives repeated notes also change the take.
- **Chords.** With `clean` and `edge`, full chords. From `crunch` up, the rule of the distorted banks holds more strongly, since the notes now really interact: root, fifth and octave, or a major triad; thirds, sevenths and added notes on another part.
- **Two players.** Two parts panned apart, each with its own `amp`, its own `musiclib.expression` seed and `feel`; the same voice on both, or `drive` a little apart.
  - Two rhythm guitar tracks panned to the sides are the standard of Herbst's (2017a) tracks from the 1970s to the 1990s; in his death metal tracks of the 2000s and modern extreme metal tracks of the 2010s, four "seem required" (two parts a side here). The small differences of timing between the takes are "a crucial element" of the layered sound; like sounds on both sides give a balanced wall, unlike ones a wider image. Four tracks are less defined between 500 Hz and 5 kHz than two.
- **How much drive.** More distortion joins the notes and evens their volumes, and it shrinks accents and the differences between ways of playing. Too little gives a lead that is percussive and staccato where a voice-like line is wanted; too much diminishes the effect of phrasing and dynamics, and costs clarity (the last after Mynett 2012). Herbst (2017b) finds it sensible to set the gain by the needs of the song, by section or by phrase if need be. A part's `drive` is one number: a section that wants another is written on a second part. In one amplifier channel, more distortion also tends to scoop the mids (Herbst 2017a).
- **Filters.** A guitar loudspeaker's stated range is about 75 Hz to 5 kHz (Celestion's specifications), and the chain's loudspeaker filter falls away from there (on `crunch`, 8 kHz comes out 18 dB under 2 kHz), so the `lowpass` that takes the fizz off the distorted banks (The mix, below) is not needed; the `highpass` against the bass still is.
- **The receipt** (`stem_receipts.json`, `amp`) gives `input_peak`: near 1 where the hardest chord drove the chain at its working level, and how much above or below for a part written louder or softer. A line of single notes reads well under 1 (0.37 and 0.45 on two leads rendered here) and needs no correction: for more distortion turn `drive`, not `input_db`.
- **Effects** written on the part (`effects`, `delay`, the reverb sends) come after the amplifier, as in a studio. For a clean part the starting points given for `fsbs_clean` below hold: `{"type": "chorus", "depth": 0.4}` (0.3 to 0.5) and a send to the delay.
- **By string.** Give the part its `strings` (`[40, 45, 50, 55, 59, 64]`; drop D lowers the first to 38) and each note its `string` (1 the highest), as tablature does; with the builder, `part.shape(bar, beat, [(6, 0), (5, 2), (4, 2)], 0.5)` is an E power chord. A string's note then stops when that string is struck again and the other strings ring on, a strum runs across the strings in order, and one pitch can sound on two strings at once when the two notes are written two ticks or more apart (written closer they are refused, and a strum's timing does not change that: write one of them a sixty-fourth of a beat later). Write a note no longer than to the next note on its string; the builder's `fret` and `shape` shorten a note written to ring when its string is struck again. With the builder the open strings may be written by name: `strings=["E2", "A2", "D3", "G3", "B3", "E4"]`.
  - `"mute": "palm"` on a note is the palm-muted chug: the recording damped as the hand's edge on the bridge damps it. Write the chugs and the open accents in one part, at their written lengths, the accents louder; the separate muted part with short gates (below) is for the amplified banks.
  - `"mute": "dead"` is the click of a string stopped by the fretting hand: the strokes between chords in sixteenth-note strumming, and the string between the two notes of an octave shape.
  - An upstroke usually catches only the upper strings: leave the lowest ones out of it.
  - The damping's times are this skill's choices, not measurements of a muted guitar. Set beside a published measurement of palm-muted against open playing through distorted amplifiers (Herbst 2017a), the mute on `high_gain` agrees with it within about 3 dB from 250 Hz up; on `crunch` and the cleaner voices it takes more from the top than his average, 4 dB more at 1 to 2 kHz and 15 to 20 dB more at 6 to 10 kHz (measured here on his passage; no published figure for a clean mute was read).
  - `review` prints one line for the part (`By string, ...`): the notes on each string, the frets used, the muted notes, and the widest stretch of frets between stopped notes that sound together (open strings and dead notes left out). It is a count of the score; nothing is asked, and no fingering is chosen.

The three banks below remain for their own recorded sounds.

### The amplified banks

The FSBS banks hold one recording each, B1–D6:
- `fsbs_dist1`: a Fender through an amp and effects, darker;
- `fsbs_dist2`: brighter;
- `fsbs_clean`.

They have no palm-muted, harmonic or slide samples. The points below write those effects around the recordings.

- **Power chords.** Root, fifth and octave in the low octave, B1–B2, for the rhythm parts; D2–A2–D3 in drop D. On the other side, a voicing an octave higher (the octave and the fifth above it) thickens a chorus.
- **Two players, panned apart.** Rock and metal production records the rhythm guitar part at least twice, as separate performances panned apart. Mynett (2017) compares double tracking with quad tracking, two takes per side.
  - Write two parts, one on `fsbs_dist1` and one on `fsbs_dist2`, panned −0.9 and +0.9 or wider.
  - Give each its own `musiclib.expression` seed and `feel`.
  - A copy of one part only moves the image; it does not double.
- **Palm-muted chugs.** With the picking hand damping the strings, the notes come out shorter and duller. Put them on a part of their own:
  - root and fifth, or the root alone;
  - short notes (`gate` about 0.4);
  - velocities 12–16 below the open chords, accents on the beat;
  - a lower `lowpass` than the open guitars (6–8 kHz), and `highpass` about 90 Hz.

  The open, accented chords stay on the other parts at full length.
- **Strumming.** For chords on and between the beats, `musiclib.expression` with `{"strum": 4–6}` ([performance-and-mix.md](performance-and-mix.md)). Chugs and gallops are picked down on every note, and the strum spread hardly matters on two or three strings.
- **Clean guitars.** `fsbs_clean` for arpeggios and full chords, with `effects` `chorus` (`depth` 0.3–0.5) and a delay for space.
- **The lead.** `fsbs_dist2`, centred or slightly off centre:
  - `performance.legato`, so connected notes slur as hammer-ons and pull-offs do;
  - bends into long notes, vibrato on held notes and slides between positions (`bend_range` 12; [performance-and-mix.md](performance-and-mix.md));
  - a `delay` of a dotted eighth (0.75 beats) about 18 dB down, and the plate.
- **Twin leads.** Traditional heavy metal harmonises its guitars, and melodic death metal makes harmonised riffs its melody (Wikipedia, Iron Maiden; Melodic death metal). Write the second lead a diatonic third or sixth from the first and pan the two apart.
- **Scales**, from the style articles (Wikipedia, in Sources):
  - minor pentatonic and the blues scale for rock leads, and in traditional heavy metal solos over Aeolian and Phrygian riffs;
  - chromatic, tritone-heavy lines for thrash;
  - natural and harmonic minor, with neoclassical arpeggios, for power and melodic metal, whose fast and technical solos are close to a rule.

## Bass

- **With the riff.** The gallop sits in the guitars and the bass, often doubled by the kick (Wikipedia, Iron Maiden; Heavy metal music). Write the bass with the riff an octave below the guitars, or on roots in the kick's rhythm.
- **Banks.**
  - `bass_pick`: a picked Yamaha RBX, D1–B♭2, one velocity layer. Set `performance.round_robin`, so that a note repeated within 1.2 s plays a neighbouring key's recording instead of the same one (not with bends of the part's own, and not on a part played by string, which refuses it: [schema.md](schema.md)).
  - `bass_darkblack` 0:33: fingers, five strings down to B0, four dynamics and round robins. `mix.saturate` 0.2–0.4 adds grit (a starting value of this skill).
- **Through an amplifier.** `"amp": {"voice": "bass", "drive": 0.5}` in the part's `mix` is the guitar's chain ([above](#the-direct-recording-through-an-amplifier-mixamp)) with a bass's filters: the low strings pass, the loudspeaker's range is the one a maker states for bass guitar loudspeakers (40 or 45 Hz to 3.5 kHz: Celestion's specifications), and what lies under 250 Hz is lowered ahead of the curve and raised again after it, so that more `drive` bends a note's harmonics more than its fundamental.
  - Measured here on an 82 Hz tone at the working level: at `drive` 0 the harmonics lie 26 dB under the fundamental, at 0.5 13 dB, at 1 9 dB. The voice's numbers are this skill's choices, starting points to be changed by ear.
  - The levels of `bass_pick` 0:0, `bass_finger` 0:0 and `bass_darkblack` 0:33 into the chain are measured (their hardest single note at the working level), so velocity drives it as on the guitar. `bass_darkblack` has four dynamics: measured here, its notes at velocity 64 peak some 25 dB under those at 127, so they hardly drive the chain.
  - Use `bass` for a bass, not a guitar voice: those lower what lies under a guitar's range (on `crunch`, measured here, a soft 41 Hz tone comes out 30 dB under one at 165 Hz).
  - The banks' makers do not say whether the basses were recorded from the pickup or from a loudspeaker; the voice was not made for one or the other.
- **By string.** A bass takes `strings` as a guitar does (`[28, 33, 38, 43]`; a five-string `[23, 28, 33, 38, 43]`), with `"mute": "palm"` and `"dead"` on its notes, and without `performance.round_robin`.
- **Registers.** Keep the bass mostly below C3, and high-pass the guitars above it (The mix, below).

## Arrangement

| Part | Verse | Pre-chorus | Chorus | Solo or bridge | Breakdown (metalcore) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drums | backbeat, closed hi-hat | toms, a building snare, a fill | crash and ride, or the double kick | the chorus groove, or half time | half time; the kick with the chugs; the china |
| Bass | roots with the kick | climbing | the riff, or roots in eighths | the chord roots | the chugs, with the kick |
| Rhythm guitars | one guitar, muted or clean | the second guitar enters | both sides, open power chords | held chords | open-string chugs on both sides |
| Lead | the melody low, or the riff | rising | the hook, high | the solo | silent, or one held high note |

The table is an example plan. Build by addition toward the chorus (McAdams, Goodchild and Soden 2022 describe such gradual addition), and hold layers back so the last chorus has somewhere to go (Senior 2024).

## The mix

- **The low end.** The kick, the bass and the guitars' low strings overlap there.
  - High-pass the rhythm guitars: start about 80 Hz and sweep up, to about 100–120 Hz in standard tuning and closer to 80–100 Hz for drop tunings and seven-strings, higher where the bass carries the low end (Nail The Mix). Set the octave-up part higher still. `review` notes that this takes several dB off a low string's fundamental (D2 is 73 Hz); that is intended when the bass doubles the riff an octave below.
- **Kick definition.** A fast double kick loses its separate hits without its click, the beater's attack. Metal mixers lift it somewhere in 2–8 kHz: 2–4 kHz for a punchy thwack, 5–8 kHz for a modern click (Nail The Mix).
- **Guitars wide, the rest centred.** Double-tracked rhythm guitars sit apart, left and right (Mynett 2017); the kick, snare, bass and lead are conventionally centred (Flint 2005).
- **Fizz.** Distortion's harsh fizz sits in the highest octaves. A `lowpass` on the rhythm guitars tames it: start at 10–12 kHz and roll it down until the fizz goes (Nail The Mix).
- **Reverb.** This skill's starting point: the rhythm guitars dry or in a short room (`reverb_db` −24 or lower), the lead on the plate and the delay. Parts at the back of a mix are more reverberant than parts in front (White 2009).
- **Brightness.** Cymbals and distorted guitars put far more energy above 2.5 kHz than strings or piano do. `analyze` should show it ([anime-opening.md](anime-opening.md)).
- **Loudness.** Use the bus chain with `glue` at −14 LUFS, Spotify's playback level ([pop-and-ballad.md](pop-and-ballad.md)).
- **The lead's level.** Check `lead_minus_accompaniment` in `analyze`, and find what masks the lead by muting the other parts one at a time (Senior 2018).

## Symptoms and causes

| Symptom | Likely causes | What to check or change |
|---|---|---|
| Small guitars | one rhythm part; a copied part; thirds in the distorted chords | two parts played separately and panned hard; power chords |
| Muddy low end | guitars, bass and kick all below 150 Hz; too much reverb on the guitars | the guitars high-passed; one octave for the bass; dry rhythm guitars |
| Mushy chugs | long gates; chugs on the open-chord part; reverb | a muted part of its own with short gates and a lower `lowpass`; no reverb |
| A machine-gun double kick | one velocity; no click | alternating velocities; the kick's 2–8 kHz |
| Riffs that do not hit | the guitars, bass and kick not in unison; loose timing | the same rhythm in all three; small deviation |
| The lead disappears | the lead in the rhythm guitars' register; too little level | the lead an octave higher; delay and plate; `lead_minus_accompaniment` |
| Generic | the same chug and the same backbeat throughout | a riff of its own as the hook; a breakdown or a half-time section; a chord change on an unexpected beat |
